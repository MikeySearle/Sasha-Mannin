Every pot is a record of its own firing.

Wood-fired stoneware. Glazes mixed from ash gathered on the coast near Aberdeen.

The part she cannot control is the part that matters.

She mixes her own glazes from wood ash gathered where she lives, then fires with wood, which lays down more ash again. The last firing ran fifteen hours, stoked in shifts with a firing group near her on the Aberdeenshire coast.

Ash settles where the flame carries it. Unglazed clay takes its colour from the fire. The same glaze comes out differently depending on where a pot stood in the kiln. None of it repeats.

The potter

Sasha Mannin

Sasha Mannin works in wood-fired stoneware on the coast near Aberdeen, in a small community with the sea on one side and open country on the other. The studio is in her own home.

She fires with wood, mostly, alongside a firing group in her area; the last firing ran fifteen hours, stoked in shifts. The glazes are her own, mixed from ash she gathers where she lives, so the surface of a pot carries something of the ground it was made on.

What happens in the kiln is not hers to control. Ash carried through the chamber settles where the flame takes it and melts into the glaze; unglazed clay flashes orange and grey where the fire has run over it. Where a piece stands in the kiln decides what comes out of it. She works towards that moment rather than against it.

She is always studying — a technique, a glaze, a different way of packing a kiln — and she teaches, mostly beginners. The pots are made as art and built to be used.

Fifteen hours at the kiln, and the fire decides the rest.
